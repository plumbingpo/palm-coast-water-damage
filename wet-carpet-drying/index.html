Wet Carpet Drying: What Palm Coast Homeowners Should Know

There is always more water in a soaked carpet than the surface suggests. The face fibers can feel only damp while the pad underneath is soaked like a sponge and the subfloor below it is wet too. What you can pull out with towels or a shop vac comes from the top, not from below. For Palm Coast homes, our crew handles the extraction, meters the carpet, pad and subfloor, and runs drying gear that gets to the bottom layer.

Whether the carpet can be saved depends on two things, how clean the water was and how long it has been wet. If the water came out of a clean supply line and we reach it fast, the carpet can normally stay down while it dries. Gray water from an appliance often means the pad goes and the carpet gets cleaned and treated. Both layers, carpet and pad, are removed when the water was sewage or came in from outside flooding.

Signs to Look For

  • Carpet that still feels cold and clammy to bare feet days after the spill
  • Paint blistering near the floor, or baseboard trim that has puffed up
  • Socks that come away damp, or a squishing sound, when you cross the carpet
  • Backing that separates from the carpet when you lift a corner to look
  • Ripples or loose spots where the carpet has let go of the tack strip
  • Yellow or brown discoloration rising to the fiber tips while the carpet dries
The Clock

What Happens if You Wait

First hours

Water Moves Outward

Under the surface, the pad pulls water outward, well beyond what looks wet. It slips beneath walls into neighboring rooms while the tack strip begins to absorb it.

24 to 48 hours

Smell and Mold Start

That sour wet carpet odor shows up as bacteria multiply in the damp pad. Mold can start to grow on dust caught deep in the pile and on the carpet back.

Day 3 to 7

The Carpet Breaks Down

Ripples appear and the carpet delaminates because the latex in its backing is breaking down. Nails in the tack strip start rusting, and the subfloor swells.

After a week

Past Saving

By now the carpet and pad usually have to come out. Mold has often reached the subfloor and the bottom of the drywall, so the job grows.

What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Clean Water Turns Dirty

Water that started clean does not stay that way. It collects bacteria and dirt from the pad and fibers, which can turn a simple drying job into a removal.

Water Held in the Pad

A carpet pad absorbs many times what it weighs in water. The carpet over it and the floor under it keep getting damp from the pad, even once the surface seems dry.

Mold Under the Carpet

Mold tends to start underneath, on the carpet back, because that side stays damp and dark. Your nose finds it well before anything shows on the surface.

Damp Base of the Walls

The base of each wall is where water moving beneath the carpet ends up. It wicks up into the baseboards and drywall unnoticed, since the carpet hides that edge.

Our Process

What Happens After You Call

1

Map the Wet Area

We trace how far the water traveled with moisture meters and thermal imaging, including under walls and into closets. The water category gets identified as well, since it determines what we can keep.

2

Extract the Water

With weighted extractors, carpet and the pad beneath it are pressed and emptied in one pass. A wand by itself leaves much more behind, so this step cuts the drying time afterward.

3

Dry All the Layers

The carpet is either pulled back so the pad can go, or left down with air blown beneath it, whichever the job needs. Air movers and dehumidifiers run until carpet and subfloor both read dry.

4

Clean and Reset

Where needed, an antimicrobial goes on the subfloor and carpet, then new pad is laid if the old was removed, and the carpet is stretched back on its tack strip and cleaned.
